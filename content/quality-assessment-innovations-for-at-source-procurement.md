# Quality assessment innovations for at-source procurement
*Categories: Science, Innovation · By Daniel Gregg · 15 Sep 2025 · archived from intersectiontraders.com.au/posts/quality-assessment-innovations-for-at-source-procurement*

Coffee quality assessment is something speciality coffee supply chain actors know intimately — epitomised in the 'Q-grader' initiative and SCA parameters (www.sca.coffee). Most quality assessment comes at the finished green bean stage or after roasting. There remains little in the way of formal quality assessment for early stages of the supply chain.

Key characteristics of the early stages (in smallholder dominated settings):

- Households use labour (household or employed) to pick coffee trees
- Households sell both 'fresh' coffee cherries and semi-processed 'parchment' to middlemen, buying agents and processing stations
- Parchment quality is hard to assess without processing, roasting and cupping — creating a 'market for lemons' in localised parchment markets (see the 'good intentions…' post)
- Assessing quality of freshly picked cherries is relatively straightforward: all berries should be red across the whole fruit; holes/insect damage indicates major faults; yellow/green cherries impact flavour negatively but generate fewer 'bad' flavours
- But there are constraints: up to 100,000 berries in 100 kg makes assessing a whole lot impossible; coffee is sold by weight and selective picking costs time, so sellers face incentives to hide unripe/damaged berries under red ones, and contracting for selectively picked coffee requires additional compensation
- Commodity programs' quality assessment is highly subjective, effortful, and generally unable to distinguish average from good lots.

## Our research on quality

Our early research focused on obtaining high quality fresh coffee on the open market (maximising participation). Local growers and buyers tended to blame pickers ("we tell them to pick red but they don't know how") — an 'education' framing. We viewed it as a lemon-market problem: no actor had an incentive to pay more for quality because their upstream buyers would not pay more, so pickers were not incentivised to pick red. Picking red is less a cognitive issue ('how') and more an effort-reward issue ('why', when unpaid).

We ran field experiments testing: (1) quality incentives alone (bonus for passing a quality test); (2) 'education' alone; (3) both. Results showed very high quality outcomes from (1) alone – contracting for quality. There was no 'education' issue; pickers just needed an incentive to take more care. Education played a small early role, quickly dominated by incentivised contracts.

We also tested post-harvest sorting: after buying ~50 kg, six of us sat sorting red berries from yellow/green/damaged. After 2 hours we were less than halfway through. Post-harvest sorting was not feasible without massive capital investment (which conflicts with keeping capital costs low for inclusivity).

## The quality-conditional contract

From these experiments we developed the pass/fail contract at the centre of our program:

- If your coffee passes our quality assessment test we offer to buy it at a premium, up-front, cash value
- If it fails, we show you why and encourage you to come back another day — but do not buy the coffee

## The sampling mechanism and the Quality Assessment Table (QAT)

We turned to sampling theory to make assessments low cost, transparent, and robust: sample a very small portion of the lot (cost-effective); assess transparently (capability building and trust); ensure the sample reflects overall lot quality with high accuracy. We landed on an approach with very high reliability up to ~150–200 kg lots:

- Remove coffee from its bag onto a sheet/platform allowing vigorous mixing (so the sample is representative)
- Sample using a small standardised container
- Score 'bad': count only 'black' berries (2 points) and 'yellow' berries (1 point); if the score hits 20 the lot fails

The negative score reduces assessment cost by focusing on the smaller set of failure items, which also stand out visually.

We drew up a concept for a 'quality assessment table' (QAT) and took it to a local fabricator: a platform box table with short sides, a hinge and funnel at one end, painted red (the red surface helps identify non-target colours immediately). The process:

1. A prospective seller arrives; 2. We pour their coffee (up to 150 kg) onto the table; 3. Mix uniformly; 4. Sample; 5. Score bad (1pt yellow, 2pt black); 6. Pour the coffee back into the bag; 7. If it passes, weigh and offer an entirely voluntary, non-binding sale contract; 8. If it fails, explain the failure mechanism and suggest returning with a small lot until confident — lowering effort risk and growing learning.

The whole process typically takes less than five minutes (not counting chit chat!) — far more efficient than any current quality assessment mechanism we've come across, and far more robust. Tying these together gave us the kernel of an inclusive, high-value coffee supply program; next, mechanisms to enable scaling (digitisation in payment and quality assurance).
