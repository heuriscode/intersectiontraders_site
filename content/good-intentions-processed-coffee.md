# Good intentions… why not help growers themselves sell processed coffee in smallholder settings?
*Categories: Evidence, Science · By Daniel Gregg · 15 Sep 2025 · archived from intersectiontraders.com.au/posts/good-intentions-why-not-help-growers-themselves-sell-processed-coffee-in-smallholder-settings*

In partnership with a PhD student at Göttingen University in Germany working with us we looked into a common suggestion purporting to increase value to household coffee growers – that they engage in processing of coffee to produce 'parchment' instead of selling fresh coffee berries.

*Note: this brief is based on our published paper: Arslan, C., Gregg, D., & Wollni, M. (2024). Paying more to make less: value degrading in the coffee value chain in eastern Uganda. American Journal of Agricultural Economics, 106(1), 96-117. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajae.12389*

Value chain upgrading is the process of seeking to increase one's benefits within a value chain by moving to capture 'higher value' within the supply chain. One of the most common methods is 'functional upgrading' wherein an actor adopts processes used by upstream actors — e.g. a livestock farmer installing an abattoir to sell processed meat. This 'vertical integration' can work well where the market for processed goods is well-established and has no quality assurance issues.

Within the eastern Ugandan highlands this idea was regularly promoted as a way for households to realise more income: processing fresh coffee berries at home to create 'parchment' coffee. Ostensibly a good idea if parchment coffee were worth sufficiently more than the *equivalent* value of fresh coffee. But it is not.

*Parchment (home-processed coffee) is actually worth the same or less than the equivalent volume of 'fresh' coffee sold. One must also account for additional costs in processing at home, including capital investment (a coffee 'grater', barrels for fermentation, tarpaulins/tables for drying). Parchment also takes 2–6 weeks to dry before being sellable.*

Our research showed that this value shortfall resulted in the majority of households that undertook home processing having lower unit revenues (per kilogram of equivalent fresh coffee) compared to those selling fresh berries. The only cases indicating a possible (not guaranteed) benefit were: (1) households far from buying agents, for whom selling fresh coffee was difficult; and (2) relatively sophisticated sellers who sold 'good' coffee fresh and reserved 'bad' coffee for parchment.

The latter exploits the fact that parchment quality is far harder to discern than fresh-cherry quality — generating a 'market for lemons' in which the parchment market is inherently low value and low quality with no potential for quality differentiation.

Our research shows the importance of not taking ideas around ethics as 'obvious' – they typically are not. Our work continually seeks to generate the insights we need to innovate in supply chain operations to support truly ethical outcomes.
