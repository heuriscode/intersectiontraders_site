# Ethical 'sales chains': ideas and concepts for Intersection Traders
*Category: Innovation · By Daniel Gregg · 15 Sep 2025 · archived from intersectiontraders.com.au/posts/ethical-sales-chains-ideas-and-concepts-for-intersection-traders*
*Status note: these are ideas/concepts for future trials — not an operating program.*

Coffee folks talk a lot about the ethics of their supply chains. This refers to the procurement of green or roasted coffee beans from green bean traders and/or from processors based in source countries. For the most part, these ethical claims are just that – claims with little to no basis in reality.

Few coffee retailers or roasted coffee suppliers talk about the ethics of their *sales* chains. These are the activities, after roasting of coffee, that provide you with your coffee whether that is in roasted bean format or in your cup of coffee from your favourite café. As with supply chains, ethics in sales chains are not 'automatic'. Just because you are not unethical, doesn't mean that your sales chain is ethical. So how can we 'do' better in sales chains?

We view important goals in our sales chain as being:

- To generate opportunities for disadvantaged individuals to gain benefits from playing roles in our sales chain
- To ensure that our recruitment process for sales chain activities is inclusive — it includes target disadvantaged cohorts and measures success in terms of that inclusion and the associated benefits it generates for those people
- To ensure that our sales chain program is scalable, financially sustainable and cost effective to maximise its resilience and ability to generate benefits far into the future.

Approaches considered:

| Approach | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional model:** standard sales chain with a large centralised roastery (wholesale to cafes/offices, retail bagged beans via post/shopfront), employing people with disability in the roastery. | Straightforward; proven sales approaches; very strong quality control via centralised roasting. | Low scalability – limited employment opportunities; existing sales chains are extremely competitive with limited ability to generate value; high risk. |
| **Catchment micro-entrepreneurs:** distributed sales chain with catchments based on online sales, supported by people with disability as micro-entrepreneurs who package, label and distribute coffee to customers. | Potentially highly scalable in sales and participation; high potential value to participants plus community engagement; potential for an investable, independent social enterprise in Australia that also supports the procurement chain through wholesale purchases at scale; highly testable at low cost; very strong quality control via centralised roasting. | New concept requiring testing (operations and demand); marketing to shift purchasing behaviour is critical early; requires a unique approach to web-based sales (minor). |
| **Catchment micro-roasters:** distributed small roasters targeting groups (people with disability, community groups) as micro business owners running catchment-level distribution. | Potentially highly scalable; flexibility to target community-focused groups. | Substantial quality-assurance difficulty (roasting is critical to quality); same risks as micro-entrepreneur model; substantial training and capital per micro-roaster; emerging, costly technology. |

Our view is that the second option, catchment-based micro-entrepreneurs, presents the most viable and high impact pathway for a future sales chain innovation. We'll be looking to trial this and will be seeking social impact investment partners to assist in an initial trial and then to grow it to a pilot program. We have developed the business case for this approach – low risk, low to no investment costs, substantial potential upside to micro-entrepreneur partners and social impact investment partners.
