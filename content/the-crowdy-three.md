# The 'crowdy three' – a conceptual model of how supply chains can (should) act as development initiatives (i.e. be 'ethical')
*Categories: Science, Innovation, Evidence · By Daniel Gregg · 15 Sep 2025 · archived from intersectiontraders.com.au/posts/the-crowdy-three-a-conceptual-model-of-how-supply-chains-can-should-act-as-development-initi-atives-ie-be-ethical*

*Note: This post is derived from the joint work of Dr Daniel Gregg and Dr Daniel Hill in relation to mechanisms for market-based programs to act as effective development initiatives. See www.heuris.com.au. Note 2: This is a longer post…*

Value chain development remains a key tool to achieve socio-economic objectives in rural areas of all countries. Yet value chain development at scale is difficult. Bulk commodity supply chain programs remain low-value for participating farming households on average, undermining potential improvements in revenue for smallholder farmers and economic benefit for source countries. Overall quality, even of the growing array of 'specialty' commodity programs in, for example, coffee and cocoa, remains low despite the promise of 'relationship trade' models and the growing range of certification standards supposed to increase value to smallholder farmers. Even when these specialty programs generate increased value there is increasing evidence to show that they fail to do so in a way that benefits target households (i.e. they do so for the 'elites' in a community, rather than for poor households most in need of income improvements).

We introduce conceptual, and pragmatic, solutions to these issues. The core problem facing achievement of high-value and financially sustainable smallholder inclusive value chains (SIVCs) is conceptualised as needing simultaneous achievement of three outcomes that tend to crowd each other out in traditional supply chain models: (1) value to participating farming households; (2) inclusivity (effective pro-poor targeting for participation), and; (3) scalability (the program is able to substantively impact on national-level welfare outcomes). We term these three concerns the 'crowdy three'.

## Poverty and the role of 'value chain upgrading'

Extreme poverty, measured by the number of individuals living below $1.90 US dollars a day, remains high at 689 million people (World Bank, 2020). There is an increasing consensus that economic growth is insufficient in alleviating poverty where inequalities often prevent the poorest and most marginalised accessing the benefits of economic growth.

It is recognised that improving the welfare of rural communities who depend on agriculture is the most important goal in global poverty alleviation. However, modern agricultural supply chains have evolved over the last 20 years due to worldwide income growth, technological change, urbanisation, economic liberalisation, and globalisation. Evidence suggests that this evolution has made smallholders and poorer rural communities more vulnerable to volatile prices and has favoured power imbalances in supply chains where rents are extracted to downstream retailers and processors.

Value chain upgrading is the process of improving the competitiveness of the value chain by improving product quality and production efficiency, or changing value chain functions, governance or end markets (Humphrey and Schmitz 2002). It has been adopted by organisations such as the FAO (Kelly et al. 2015) and UNDP (2015), and represents a key focus area for large NGOs (Oxfam-International 2019) and ACIAR (2020). Smallholder dominated, export orientated value chains, such as coffee, cocoa, vanilla, and other tree crops have received particular attention given production is closely linked to a large number of poorer communities (Vicol et al. 2018, Ros-Tonen et al. 2019). Upgrading in such chains is often achieved through differentiation along product attributes based on quality, ethical or sustainable "content" (Ponte 2002, Reardon et al. 2009).

## Empirical evidence suggests many value chain interventions fail to achieve goals

A significant number of studies have considered whether value chain upgrading processes provide broader development outcomes for rural communities (see Oya 2012, Ton et al. 2018, Bellemare and Bloem 2018, Oya et al. 2018 and German et al. 2020 for reviews). Evidence suggests that value chain upgrading is often strongly associated with 'elite capture' wherein wealthier and/or more socially powerful households are able to capture opportunities more effectively than poorer households (Vicol et al. 2018, German et al. 2020). Whilst these households are often able to derive value from participation through higher incomes or new market opportunities, this value is concentrated only within a few households who are relatively well off.

In some cases value chain upgrading avoids this elite capture issue, but the value of participation in terms of average income has been shown to be lower (Ton et al. 2018) — defined in the literature as adverse inclusion, where participation leads to low or diminishing returns due to unfavourable terms of engagement (Herrmann and Grote 2015, Ros-Tonen et al. 2019). Adverse inclusion can also relate to other aspects of welfare such as reducing women's empowerment, exposing smallholders to additional risk, or trapping households in cycles of indebtedness (German et al. 2020).

Scalability, the third element, is rarely discussed in the literature. Value chains may be both inclusive and provide value for participants but, because of increasing cost of maintaining relationships across member bases to provide assurance over product quality, tend to be uncompetitive as they scale without external funding. Governance also tends to be unstable: as these value chains scale they either fail in inclusion (trend towards elite capture) or value (adverse inclusion). Platteau (2009) shows that scalability is a 'hard' problem for development programs.

## The crowdy three

- **Value:** upgrading must generate meaningful income increases for participants accounting for the additional effort/cost of participation; may also include assurance over price and support services.
- **Inclusivity:** the distribution of value across the rural community; limited barriers to entry; broad engagement with smallholders, women, youth, and those without land.
- **Scalability:** able to grow and support more people sustainably; financially self-sufficient, effective governance, competitive for end markets.

'Three is a crowd': one, or sometimes two, of these can be solved using traditional value chain upgrading models — but not all three at once.

## Which pathway is most promising?

Open market procurement systems have repeatedly solved the scalability problem and are, essentially by definition, inclusive — true IS systems. Where open market systems fail is in achieving high value: with difficult quality monitoring they devolve into 'markets for lemons' (Akerlof 1978). Three routes exist: (1) IH → fix scalability; (2) Hs → fix inclusivity and scale; (3) IS → fix value. We view option 3 as most promising: adapt existing, well-known open market systems to incorporate quality differentiation and appropriate payment for quality. The only innovation needed is an efficient, effective, and trusted quality assessment mechanism along with a contract structure that rewards quality sufficiently. This is the basis of our Smallholder Inclusive Value Chains program.

## Asymmetric information is central

Producers face costs to produce higher quality, and quality monitoring typically occurs on an aggregated basis downstream, creating free-rider incentives (Akerlof's market for lemons). To observe quality, procuring firms face substantial monitoring costs, so monitoring programs in high-quality value chains ubiquitously involve contracting with a smaller number of larger producers — the 'relationship' approach, often claimed as ethical, but which the evidence and theory indicate creates elite capture (Ton et al 2018, Vicol 2018). For value chain upgrading to be an effective development vehicle, the trade-off between information asymmetry and transaction costs needs to be resolved.

## The Smallholder Inclusive Value Chains (SIVC) project

A trial program operating since 2018 to generate substantial value from accessing, at scale, high-value coffee markets for Ugandan coffee farmers, with a novel digitisation, governance, and procurement regime. It leverages existing institutional infrastructure including cooperative unions, growers associations and NUCAFE. The trial has doubled in scale (or more) each year and has generated:

- **Value to farmers:** an increase of, on average, 20%–40% compared to normal market prices — equivalent to over 1.5 months of normal labour income, enough to send a child to school for an entire year.
- **Value retention in Uganda:** +148% vs the average Arabica coffee sold in Uganda; +49% vs the highest-grade Mt Elgon Arabica sold in 2021.
- **Smallholder focus:** an average accepted lot size of approximately 50 kg (harvestable by one individual within ~3 hours) and ~30 farmers contributing per tonne of green-equivalent coffee.
- **Inclusivity:** over half of the volume of accepted coffee provided by youth and women.
- **Scalability:** a distributed network of buying agents requiring no capital with essentially no governance issues (safety, financial accountability).
- **Financial sustainability:** a proven business model that is financially sustainable.

## Remaining gaps and next steps

1. Testing across regions for governance scalability (Platteau 2009); 2. Innovating in procurement systems (governance and QA across wide regions; coordination for quantity/quality targets; enabling a micro-entrepreneur ecosystem); 3. Enhancing traceability; 4. Increasing digitisation to reduce in-person oversight and payment needs.

We are also researching how to increase the impact of financial transfers: recent work (Cansin et al., working paper) shows income transfers often do not lead to large welfare gains due to social and kin 'taxation'. Research directions include pro-poor targeting mechanisms; increasing transfer from income to savings/productive investment (behavioural interventions; annuity/perpetuity/distributed payment structures); broadening the supply base while improving inclusivity; and setting up as a national program.
